Can I trick your brain in 30 minutes?

Five experiments. One unsuspecting audience.

Here is the deal

Over the next half hour I am going to try to fool you. Misremembering things you never heard, missing something enormous, confidently giving me answers you have no business being confident about.

We are all going to fall for most of it, and that is rather the point.

Reading is not optional

You have read so much for so long that reading now happens to you. You cannot switch it off, so it interferes with the thing you were actually asked to do.

The mechanism: two cognitive processes compete for the same response channel: the automatic one (reading) wins because it finishes first.

Stroop, 1935. Still undefeated.

Experiment three

Two halves of a room

Left half and right half. Pick your side and remember it.

Right side: eyes closed

Was Gandhi older or younger than 9 when he died?

Left side: eyes closed

Was Gandhi older or younger than 140 when he died?

So how old was he?

Best guess. Shout it out, one side at a time.

Anchor 9
Anchor 140
Actual78

A number you knew was wrong still moved you

Nobody believed Gandhi was nine. It pulled the estimate anyway, because we adjust from whatever number we encountered first, and we nearly always stop adjusting too soon.

The mechanism: the anchor becomes the starting point for a mental search, and the search stops the moment a plausible value appears.

Anchoring. Tversky and Kahneman, 1974. It is why salary negotiations, sentencing decisions and every price tag you have ever seen start with a number someone else chose.

Experiment two, continued

Which of these did you see?

Hands up. Be honest.

DREAM you saw this
PILLOW never shown
TIRED you saw this
SLEEP never shown

You remembered a word that was never there

Every word pointed at sleep, so your brain filed the gist and quietly manufactured the rest. The false memory feels exactly like the real ones. There is no wobble, no asterisk.

The mechanism: memory stores meaning and structure, not a recording, so the brain reconstructs plausible details on retrieval, and sometimes reconstructs ones that were never there.

Deese, Roediger & McDermott. The same machinery underwrites a confident, sincere and completely wrong eyewitness.

Attention is a spotlight, not a floodlight

Focusing is not turning something up; it is turning almost everything else off. What you don't attend to, you don't see, even when it is right in front of you.

The mechanism: the brain only processes unattended visual information up to a basic level; if an object doesn't trigger a priority signal, it never reaches conscious awareness.

Simons & Chabris, 1999. The people who counted most accurately are usually the ones who missed the most.

Experiment five

Something in this room changes

One thing. It is not subtle. Hand up when you see it.

It was there the whole time

In normal vision, sudden motion attracts attention automatically. Remove that transient, even for a fraction of a second, and change becomes invisible until you search for it deliberately.

The mechanism: we do not maintain a detailed visual memory between glances. We think we do, but the scene is only refreshed where attention is pointed.

Change blindness. Rensink, O'Regan & Clark, 1997.

None of this means people are stupid

These are not glitches. They are the same mechanisms that let you read instantly, remember the meaning of a conversation rather than every word, and make quick judgements under pressure.

They work beautifully in the everyday. They only look like failures in carefully constructed situations designed to exploit them: experiments, and occasionally the real world too.

Thank you

The lesson, if there is one: a brain that works efficiently is not the same as a brain that works accurately. Most of the time that is fine.
